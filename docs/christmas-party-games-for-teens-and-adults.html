Christmas party games for teens and adults in the same room

The mixed-age room is the hard one. Here is what works when the guests are 14 to 74, what to avoid, and how to run each one so it does not die after four minutes.

Written while building printable party games · updated September 2026

Most Christmas party game lists are written for one of two rooms: small children, or adults after four drinks. The room that actually exists at Christmas is neither. It is a fifteen-year-old who would rather be anywhere else, two parents, a grandparent, an uncle who will absolutely take over, and somebody's new partner who has met one person here.

Three things make a game work in that room, and a game needs all three:

  • Nobody has to perform. Charades is the classic mixed-age game and it is also the classic mixed-age disaster, because the cost of participating is standing up alone in front of people you are related to.
  • Being good at it is not about being loud. If volume wins, the uncle wins, and everybody else stops trying by round two.
  • You can join in at minute twenty. People wander off to the kitchen. A game that punishes you for missing ten minutes is a game half the room will not re-enter.

Eight that work, with the trick for each one

1. A paper-led murder mystery

The best option for this room and the least obvious one, as long as it is the right kind. Look for a game where guests read and argue rather than perform — character cards as reference sheets rather than scripts, no accents, no costumes. A fourteen-year-old who will not do a voice will happily be the person who noticed the discrepancy in the delivery log.

The trick: put the first few documents on the table before anyone sits down. People start reading with their coats still on, and that solves the cold start that kills most party games.

2. The call-my-bluff present

Everyone brings one small wrapped thing. The owner gives three explanations of what it is and why they own it; two are false. Points for guessing, points for fooling.

The trick: a two-minute limit per person, timed, out loud. Without it the uncle takes eleven minutes.

3. Guess the year

Photographs, news headlines, adverts, songs. Teams write a year on a slip and score by how close they are rather than by being exactly right.

The trick: scoring by distance is what makes it work across ages. A teenager who guesses 1985 for something from 1979 beats a grandparent who was certain it was 1968.

4. The shared crossword or cryptogram

One large printed grid on the table and everybody drifts past it all evening. Almost nobody lists this as a party game and it is the most reliably used thing on the table.

The trick: print it big. A3 if you can, or A4 at double size across two sheets.

5. Two rooms, one code

Split into two teams in different rooms with half a cipher each and a single phone call allowed. It runs on cooperation instead of confidence, which changes who is good at it.

6. Blind taste tests, non-alcoholic

Six mince pies, six chocolates, six supermarket own-brand versions of the same thing. Score out of ten on a printed sheet, reveal at the end.

The trick: make it supermarket brands rather than anything fancy. The fun is everybody being wrong about the expensive one, and it includes the fourteen-year-old and the non-drinker on identical terms.

7. The quiz with a rule that levels it

A normal quiz is won by the person who watches the news. Add the rule that each round must be answered by a different person from each team, and the team has to nominate before the question is read.

8. Escape-room-in-an-envelope

A sequence of locked puzzles where each answer opens the next. The reason it works in a mixed room is that different puzzle types suit different people: one is anagram, one is arithmetic, one is a picture, one is a map.

The trick: have a hint system decided in advance and written down, so asking for help is a rule rather than a defeat.

Four to avoid in this particular room

  • Anything where the forfeit is embarrassment. The person it lands on hardest is the teenager or the new partner.
  • Anything that needs everybody's phone. Two people will not have the app, one phone will be at 3%, and you have now spent nine minutes on setup.
  • Physical minute-to-win-it games. They are excellent and they quietly exclude anybody with a bad knee, which at a family Christmas is usually the person whose house it is.
  • Anything that requires lying. Fine with friends, oddly uncomfortable across generations. Games built on omission rather than deception land better.

How to run the evening, not just choose the game

  1. Start before you start. One thing on the table people can pick up while the food is happening. This is the single biggest improvement available to any party.
  2. One main game, not four. Four short games is three cold starts.
  3. Put the timings on alarms. If the host is also the timekeeper, the host is not at the party.
  4. Print more score sheets than you need. Always.
  5. End on something written. A secret ballot, a folded guess, a scored sheet. It gives you an ending to announce instead of a game that peters out because somebody has to put the turkey carcass in the bin.

A note on the fourteen-year-old

The usual advice is to give teenagers a job. It is right, but the job matters. Handing them the music or the camera keeps them busy and outside the game. Give them something inside it instead: the person who holds the evidence envelopes and opens them on the clock, the one who counts the ballots, the one who reads the ending out loud. Those are jobs with authority, they require no performance, and they are the reason a teenager stays at the table.
