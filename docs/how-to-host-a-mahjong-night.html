How to host a mahjong night

Four players, three hours, one table, and the six decisions that make the difference between a group that meets once and a group that meets every fortnight for nine years.

Written while making printable kits for mahjong nights · updated September 2026

Mahjong nights are one of the few social formats that genuinely sustain themselves. A book club needs somebody to have read the book. A dinner party needs somebody to cook. A mahjong night needs a table and four people who said yes, and the reason it works is that the game gives everybody something to do with their hands and a reason to come back next time.

Almost all of the hosting effort is front-loaded into decisions you make once.

1. Four, and what to do about the fifth

American mahjong is a four-player game. Five people is the most common real-world situation and there are three good answers: one person sits out each game and scores, or you play two shorter games with a swap at the halfway point, or you set up two tables and you are now a club.

Do decide this before anybody arrives. A fifth person standing behind a chair for three hours is the reason people do not come back.

2. The table, the light, and the chairs

You need a table you can reach across from four sides, so square beats rectangular. Thirty-two to thirty-six inches square is about right; a dining table works if it is not too wide.

The light matters more than anything else in this section. Tile faces are small, engraved and often unhelpfully coloured, and the card is worse. Overhead light plus one lamp, and nothing behind anybody's head. Half the "I cannot read this" problems at a mahjong table are lighting problems, not eyesight problems.

And a tablecloth or a felt: it quiets the tiles, which sounds trivial until you have shuffled 152 tiles on bare wood six times in an evening while somebody is talking.

3. The house rules, agreed before the first tile

This is the single most useful thing a host does, and it takes ninety seconds. Tables genuinely differ on all of these, none of them is wrong, and every one of them causes an argument if it comes up mid-hand for the first time:

  • Are we playing for money, and how much is a hand worth?
  • Does a jokerless hand pay extra?
  • Do we allow pass-backs in the courtesy pass?
  • Which passes may be blind?
  • What happens to a dead hand — does the player keep playing for defence, or stop?
  • How much table talk is allowed? Some tables call discards out loud; some do not.

Say all six out loud at the start of the evening, especially if anybody is new. Nobody has ever minded being asked.

4. The new player

There is almost always one. Three things help more than patience does:

  • Give them a reference sheet of their own, so they are not asking the table which way the Charleston goes for the fourth time. People stop asking long before they stop needing to know, which is when they start quietly having a bad evening.
  • Play the first hand face up. The whole table open, talking through what each person is building. It costs one hand and it saves an evening.
  • Tell them to pick three hands, not learn the card. See the beginner's guide.

5. Food that does not touch the tiles

This is a real constraint and not a joke. Tiles get handled constantly by four people, and anything buttery, oily, powdery or sticky ends up on them and then on the card.

What works: things on sticks, things in bowls that are eaten between games rather than during, and a designated break. What does not: crisps, anything with a dip, and cake with icing.

The simplest structure is to play two games, break properly for twenty minutes with real food away from the table, and then play two more. It also gives people a natural place to leave.

6. Make it recur before anybody goes home

The whole return on hosting is the fortnightly habit, and the habit is created in the ninety seconds after the last hand. Fix the next date while people are putting their coats on, in the room, out loud. Not "we should do this again". A date.

A rotating host is better than a fixed one, if anybody else has a table. And a running score kept across sessions — even a silly one — is disproportionately effective, because it turns four evenings into one thing with a shape.

What a good mahjong night is actually for

It is worth saying plainly, because it changes how you host one. Nobody keeps coming back for three years because the deduction is thrilling. They come back because there is a standing appointment with the same three people, with something to do with your hands so the conversation can go wherever it wants, and a small reason to care about the next hand.

Which means the hosting job is not really about mahjong. It is about lighting, seating, the break, and the date for next time.
