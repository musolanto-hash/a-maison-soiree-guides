How to host a murder mystery dinner party at home

The evening is decided by two things almost nobody thinks about when they buy the game: whether the host is allowed to play, and what happens when somebody arrives at nine. Here is how we plan one, and why.

Written while building printable mystery games · updated September 2026

We design these things for a living, which mostly means reading what went wrong at other people's parties. Spend a week in the reviews of printable murder mystery games and the same four sentences come back: the host knew the ending, somebody was late and it broke, the characters had nothing to do, and the font was too small to read in candlelight. None of those are storytelling problems. They are logistics problems, and you can solve every one of them before your first guest takes their coat off.

Step one: pick the kind of game, not the theme

Printable mysteries come in two shapes, and the theme on the cover tells you nothing about which one you are buying.

Scripted games give each guest a character with lines to deliver in each round. Somebody is the femme fatale and has to be one, out loud, in front of their brother-in-law. These are wonderful with the right eight people and agony with the wrong eight.

Paper-led games put the case on the table: statements, reports, receipts, a floor plan, a photograph. Guests read, compare and argue. Character cards exist, but they are reference sheets you answer from, not scripts you perform.

Before you buy, look for the answer to one question in the listing: does a guest have to say anything in character? If the listing will not tell you, assume yes. Mixed groups, family Christmases and work parties survive the paper-led kind far better, because nobody has to act and nobody has to lie to their mother-in-law.

Step two: decide whether you are playing

This is the decision that ruins the most evenings, because it gets made accidentally. In a lot of games the host is structurally the referee: they hold the clues, they decide who learns what and when, and they settle arguments, which means they have to know the answer, which means they cannot play.

If you want to play, you need a game where four separate things are true, and it is worth checking all four rather than trusting the phrase "the host can play too" on the cover:

  • The host's required reading is short. One page, not a plot summary.
  • The host does not control the flow of clues. Envelopes should open on a clock time, not when the host judges the moment is right.
  • Nothing needs adjudicating. In our games nobody lies — every card is literally true — so there is no lie-detection and therefore no referee.
  • The reveal is sealed and read out by somebody else at the end.

There is one residual risk that no game can remove and that you should just accept: you have to print the thing, and a print preview is a screen. Keep the answer in a separate file, print it without opening it, and put it in an envelope before you look at anything else.

Step three: the timetable

A dinner party mystery wants three clue drops and three eating gaps, and the clock does the work so you do not have to. This is the shape we build to, at 90 to 120 minutes for 6 to 12 people:

0:20Round one questioning. Everybody reads, everybody asks.Starter, already cold and plated

Two details matter more than the timings themselves. Put the envelope times on phone alarms before anyone arrives, so the evening is run by a phone and not by you. And cook the sort of meal that can sit: a cold starter, a main that reheats, a pudding that lives in the fridge. A mystery where the host disappears for twenty minutes to plate fish is a mystery with a hole in round two.

Step four: plan for the three things that always happen

Somebody arrives late. Do not pre-assign characters by email. Print all the cards, put them in a bowl and let people draw. A game that survives a late arrival is a game where roles are taken, not allocated, and where there is a one-paragraph card that can get somebody playing in ninety seconds.

Somebody leaves early. This is the one that quietly destroys scripted games, because a guest walking out at nine takes a third of the solution home in their coat pocket. The fix is a design rule: every fact the answer depends on has to exist in two places, on a person and on a document that stays on the table. When we audit our own games that is the whole test — in the Christmas one it is 58 facts, and all 58 have to be printed somewhere as well as spoken, including the ones on the culprit's own card.

The headcount changes on the day. Buy a game with a range, not a number, and check how it scales. The honest version is a fixed deal order: cards go out in a set sequence, and a suspect nobody is playing becomes a suspect you read about instead of talk to, because their statement is on the table either way.

Step five: the small stuff that people remember

  • Light. Candlelight is lovely and it is why "the font was too small" is the single most common complaint in this category. Print a test page and read it at arm's length, by the light you will actually have, before you print twenty more.
  • Pencils. One each, plus a shared sheet in the middle of the table that the room fills in together. The moment a party stops being polite is the moment two people start arguing over a grid in the middle of the table.
  • A secret ballot. Written, folded, counted at the end. It stops the loudest person at the table deciding the answer for everybody, and it gives you a genuine result to announce.
  • Prizes that are not just for winning. Somebody should win for being wrong in the most interesting way, and somebody should win for arriving last.

What to do in the week before

  1. Seven days out. Buy the game and read the host page only. Count your yes replies.
  2. Four days out. Print one page as a test. Read it at the table, in the evening, with the lamps you will use. If it is hard, print the large-print or low-ink edition instead.
  3. Two days out. Print everything. Sort into envelopes and write the opening time on each one. Put the answer file in its own envelope without opening it.
  4. The day. Set three alarms. Lay the table with the case file already on it — guests start reading before they have taken their coats off, and that is the best twenty minutes of the night.

Everything else is dinner, which you already know how to do.
