How many people do you need for a murder mystery party?

Four can work, six is comfortable, eight to twelve is the sweet spot, and above sixteen you are running a different kind of event entirely. What actually changes is not the fun, it is the structure.

Written while building printable mystery games · updated September 2026

The number on the box is a marketing number. What you want to know is what the evening feels like at your headcount, and what quietly breaks when you are one over or three under. Here is the honest version, written from the inside of building these things.

4–5A tight table game, 45–60 minEvery player is load-bearing unless the paper carries the answer

Four to five: possible, and the design has to earn it

With four players there is nowhere to hide. If the game distributes the solution across the guests, one person going quiet takes a quarter of it with them. The only way a four-player mystery is genuinely fair is if the documents carry the whole case and the people are the fast route rather than the only one.

That is exactly the constraint we wrote our free four-player mini mystery under: four suspects, eleven printed documents, forty-five minutes, and a solution that can be reached by a person who talks to nobody. It is free, it is complete, and it is the cheapest way to find out whether your group likes this sort of evening at all.

Six to eight: the classic, and the one that runs short

Six is the smallest number at which the table feels like a party rather than a puzzle night. The failure mode at six to eight is not boredom, it is speed: a small group reads the first envelope, solves faster than the writer expected, and the evening ends at forty minutes with an hour of lasagne left.

The thing to check before buying is whether the game has a real pacing ladder. When we read-tested our Christmas game the first time, a fresh reader named the culprit at the end of round two with 93% confidence, which meant the last third of the evening had no "who" left in it. That is a defect, not a compliment. We rebuilt round three around it — moving the shoe measurements, the salt and a scale-house ticket into a new sheet — until the end of round two had three suspects still live and the top one sitting at exactly 50%.

You cannot see any of that from the outside. What you can look for is a listing that talks about rounds in the plural and says what arrives in each one.

Nine to twelve: where these games are happiest

At ten or twelve you can afford two categories of guest, and that is what makes the evening feel full. Suspects have something to answer for. Witnesses saw one thing each and are not under suspicion, which is the right role for the friend who came for the food.

The watch-out at this size is reading load. Twenty-two documents is a lot of paper for a table that is also eating, and if the average guest reads six of them the design has to still work. Ours is built so that it does, but it is the assumption we are least certain about and we say so in our own notes.

Thirteen to sixteen: the awkward middle

This is the hardest size, and most games handle it by simply printing more character cards. That does not work, because the bottleneck at fourteen is not roles, it is attention. Nobody can hold a fourteen-person conversation, so the room splits into three clumps and each clump only hears a third of the evidence.

The fix is physical: get the case off the table and onto a wall, where three clumps can read the same document without passing it. If you are at fourteen and the game you are looking at is sold for "6–20", ask what changes at the top of that range. If the answer is "more cards", it will be thin.

Seventeen to forty: an event, not a dinner

Above sixteen everything inverts. You stop asking who plays which character and start asking what somebody does in the eleven minutes after they arrive, while still holding a drink and knowing nobody.

What a game needs at this size:

  • Roles handed out at the door, all evening. Not a seating plan. People arrive across an hour.
  • A small core and a large edge. Ours has eight people who were at the scene, six who saw something, and twenty-six drop-in roles for everybody else — plus spares, because forty means forty-three.
  • Badges. At a work party half the room does not know the other half's names. A badge with a department mark on it does more for the evening than any amount of plot.
  • An ending that fires on a clock. At forty people you will never get a silent room. The reveal has to work as an announcement to a room that is still half talking.
  • Written, secret accusations. Nobody wants to shout a name across a function room.

So what number should you invite?

Invite the number you would have invited anyway, then buy a game whose range has your number comfortably inside it rather than at the edge. A game sold for "6–12" played at twelve is a game played at its limit, and the limit is where the design assumptions are thinnest.

And build in a spare. Whatever you plan for, one person will cancel and one will bring somebody. A game that cannot absorb both of those on the day is a game that will make the evening about logistics, which is the one thing a party is not supposed to be about.
