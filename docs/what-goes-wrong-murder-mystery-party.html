What goes wrong at murder mystery parties, and how to stop it

Nine failure modes, all of them common, all of them preventable, and all of them visible in the reviews of games that are still selling well.

Written while building printable mystery games · updated September 2026

We researched this category by reading complaints. Not blog posts about how magical murder mystery nights are, but the two and three-star reviews people leave after the evening they actually had. The failures are boringly repetitive, which is good news: a repetitive failure is a preventable one.

1. The host knows the answer and cannot play

The most common structural complaint in the category. It is usually not stated as a complaint at all — it is stated as "I'd love to have played it myself next time".

Prevention: buy a game where the reveal is a separate sealed file, the clue envelopes open on clock times rather than on the host's judgement, and the host says nothing in character. Print the answer file without opening it. If you must be the host and the game is not built this way, recruit somebody who is not coming to the party to print and seal it for you.

2. The answer is on the first page of the PDF

This is a real and surprisingly frequent complaint: a buyer opens the download, and the solution is sitting on page one or in a file called "Game.pdf" along with everything else. The evening is over before it starts.

Prevention: before you open anything, look at the file list. If there is no separately named answer file, open the largest file with the preview pane off and scroll from the back. In our own builds the answer lives in one file, its first printed page is a full-page STOP card, and a script fails the build if the culprit's name or the time of death appears in anything a guest could pick up.

3. You cannot read it

Type is the single most repeated complaint in printable party games, and it is the one that makes the whole purchase useless rather than merely disappointing — a game you cannot read in the light you have is not a game. Script faces, six-point "atmospheric" small print, grey text on a tinted parchment background, words laid over a photograph.

Prevention: zoom into the preview images before buying, and run the one-sheet print test described in the printing guide. Buy games that offer a large-print edition, and take the low-ink edition seriously — it is usually the most legible version of the same pages.

4. The clues do not lead anywhere

The complaint reads: the reasoning has gaps, the official explanation feels forced, the ending tells you the answer without ever having given you a way to reach it. This is what happens when a mystery is written as a story with a twist rather than as a puzzle with a proof.

Prevention: look for a game that claims fair play and says how it checked. The mechanism that makes it true is unglamorous: a table with one row per fact the solution depends on, and a column saying which printed document carries it. Ours is 58 rows for the Christmas game and 35 for the office one, and the rule is that there is no such thing as a fact that exists only inside somebody's head.

5. Somebody has to act, and does not want to

A quarter of any given room will not perform in front of the others, and at a family Christmas or a work party that fraction goes up. The guest who is handed a character with an accent and a monologue will either do it badly and mind, or refuse and feel like they spoiled it.

Prevention: choose a paper-led game where cards are reference sheets rather than scripts, and where nothing is read aloud in character by a guest. Our longest character card is 190 words and you read off it when somebody asks you a question, which is a completely different social act from performing.

6. Somebody has to lie

Less discussed and quietly important. A lot of games hand one or more guests a card that instructs them to lie to the room, which at a family table can genuinely bother people, and which makes the game a lie-detection contest rather than a deduction.

Prevention: there is a design answer to this, and it is better than the problem it solves: make every card true and let the misdirection come from omission, refusal, honest mistake and missing context. "I don't remember" is then a legal answer rather than a cop-out, and nobody has to be dishonest with their in-laws.

7. The culprit gives it away, or is blamed for it

Someone is handed a card that says "you did it", and then spends the evening either overperforming innocence or being read like a book — and afterwards gets told they gave it away.

Prevention: games where nobody knows who the culprit is, including the person holding the culprit's card. It sounds impossible and it is not: the guilty character's card is entirely true and contains no confession, and the case is proved from other people's facts and from documents. The cost is real — that player loses the old thrill of getting away with it — and the way to pay it back is a prize at the end for the person who was it and never knew.

8. The extra characters have nothing to do

"Did need to add some lines for some of the non-important ones" is a review we kept finding. It happens when a game is written for eight and stretched to sixteen by inventing people.

Prevention: check what the extra roles are for. Witness roles should each carry one real fact that the solution uses. Drop-in roles at a big event should have a job — counting something, guarding something, being the only person who saw a particular door. A role whose card could be deleted without changing the answer is decoration.

9. It is over too early, or it never ends

Both happen. Small sharp groups solve a six-person game in forty minutes; a big loud room never arrives at a conclusion at all and the evening dribbles away.

Prevention: for the first, buy a game with a real third round rather than a reveal bolted to a second. For the second, buy a game whose ending fires on a clock time written on the board before anyone arrives, and which has a documented plan for nobody solving it — sealed hints that name nobody, and a reveal that works whether or not the room got there.

The five-minute pre-flight

  • Is there a separate answer file? Print it unopened.
  • Have I read one full document at the actual table, under the actual light?
  • Are the round-two and round-three envelopes sealed, with times written on them?
  • Are there alarms on my phone?
  • Are there ballots, pencils, and one shared sheet in the middle of the table?

Five yeses and almost nothing on this page can happen to you.
